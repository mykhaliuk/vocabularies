Design spec · handoff back to product

Speaker creation

A speaker stops being a string typed into a field and becomes a person you keep. Created where you need them — inside compose — and managed under your dictionary. Name is the only thing we ask for. Birthday is the quiet payoff: every word remembers how old they were when they said it.

The decisions

Q1 · where is a speaker born
Inline in compose

The chip row of your people, plus ＋ someone new — the same select/add mechanic collections already use. Creating a person never costs you the word you were in the middle of writing.

Q2 · what do we require
Name only

Relation and birthday are optional and stay optional forever. A speaker with just a name is a complete speaker — the meta line simply reads Theo.

Q3 · does the age move
No — it freezes with the word

The age shown on an entry is the age they were on the day it was said. That is the memory. Their age today appears only where we're talking about the person, not a word.

The object

Speaker

Owned by one user, private, never published. An entry points at a speaker by id; it does not carry the relation or the age.

The age rule

Measure the birthday against saidAt, never against today. A word said when she was 12 months old reads “12 mo” for the rest of her life. Today's date only enters the picture on the people screen, where we're describing the person rather than a moment.

One field, always set

saidAt is required and non-nullable. It is initialised to created_at at insert and edited in place — there is no fallback chain and no “unset” state to reason about. created_at stays what it is: an audit field about the row, not about the memory.

Rejected: inferring the date from clip metadata. It looked like the truest anchor — an old recording uploaded today was said back then — but capture dates are wrong often enough to matter: broken device clocks, timezone drift, messaging apps that stamp the re-encode. A confidently wrong date silently mis-ages a memory and reads as truth. created_at is at worst a little late, and the user can fix it.

Correcting the date

A derived age is only honest if the anchor is visible and fixable. The birthday is editable and repairs every word at once; the per-word date needed the same escape hatch, or a memory could sit mis-aged forever with nothing to notice it by.

  • Lives on the word screen only, below the collection tag. The feed never prints a date — it would clutter the rhythm and nobody needs it while scrolling.
  • Resting state is one quiet grey line: said 20 Sep 2025 · 12 mo · edit. It doubles as the explanation for why two words filed the same day can show different ages.
  • Editing shows a date field and the consequence in plain words — they were 12 mo. this is what the word will always say. No save button; the change lands as you pick.
  • Writes saidAt in place. Every entry already has one, so there is no create-versus-update branch.
  • A speaker with no birthday still gets the row — the date is worth keeping right on its own.

Creation flow — in compose

“Who said it” is a chip row, not an input. Selection is single and toggleable: tapping the active chip clears it.

  1. Open compose, type the word. Who said it shows every speaker you have, most recently used first, plus ＋ someone new. The order settles between sessions, never while the sheet is open — chips must not move under your thumb.
  2. Tapping a chip selects that person and closes the question. One tap is the whole flow for the common case.
  3. Tapping ＋ someone new expands a soft rose panel below the row: name, relation, birthday.
  4. Add is disabled until the name has content. On add, the person is created, the panel collapses, and their chip appears selected.
  5. Keep it posts. If no speaker is selected the entry is attributed to You — we do not block the word on the person.

Managing — “your people”

Reached from my dictionary → your people → manage, or by tapping any avatar in the people row. This screen is for repair and upkeep, not for creation in the flow of writing — though Add someone is here too, using the identical form.

  • Each row: avatar, name, relation · age today, word count. Tap opens an edit sheet prefilled with the current values.
  • A person with no relation reads no relation yet in place of the subtitle — never an empty row.
  • Word count is the archive count plus anything kept this session. Singular reads 1 word.
  • Editing a relation or a birthday propagates immediately: the feed re-derives on next render. A corrected birthday corrects every past word's age as it should have been — the freeze is on the date, not on a cached string.

Edges

CaseBehaviour
No speakers yetRow shows only ＋ someone new. First-run compose therefore teaches the mechanic without an onboarding step.
Duplicate nameAllowed — two Grandmas is a real situation. The relation line disambiguates. No warning.
No speaker chosenEntry is attributed to You. Never blocks the post.
Speaker removedTwo taps — remove from your people, then yes, remove. Their words stay; the meta line falls back to the denormalised name with no relation or age.
Many speakersChip row wraps. Beyond roughly a dozen, most-recently-used ordering carries the common case; a search affordance is a v1 problem, not v0.
Adult with a birthdayRenders 41 y. Opt-in, and some people will want it — we don't second-guess them.

Not in v0 · open for product

  • Merging duplicates. Deliberately out. Needs a reassign-all-words operation and an undo.
  • Photo avatars. Initials only for now. A real photo is the obvious next warmth upgrade.
  • Speaker page. A read-only “everything Mira has said” view. We looked at reaching it by tapping the name in the feed and dropped it — not enough value for the affordance. If it ships, the browse entry belongs in saved (alongside collections, which already carry a voices field), not in discover — discover is other people's dictionaries, and mixing private speakers in there confuses two different meanings of “people”.
  • Backfilling old entries. One migration: saidAt = created_at for every existing row. No inference, no nulls.
